Q-ify: trusted help for allowed physical queues.

A proposed Philippine marketplace connecting customers who cannot remain in line with verified runners who can hold an ordinary, transferable place at an approved venue.

Role
Product strategy, backend architecture, and implementation
Target experience
Mobile-first web
Status
Phase 1 complete - Not launched

The problem

Waiting costs more than time.

Physical queues can mean missed work, uncertain travel, heat or rain, and difficulty caring for children or relatives. Existing options either move the burden to someone else or work only when a venue has installed its own queue system.

Research and evidence

The behavior exists, but demand remains unproven.

Public research found local queue-management products, paid assistance for online ticket queues, and an informal Metro Manila offer to wait in physical lines. It did not establish transaction volume or prove a viable Philippine marketplace for physical queue handoffs.

Evidence: phone-first access is credible in the Philippines, while willingness to pay, repeat demand, and venue acceptance are still hypotheses.

The scope correction

The responsible product is narrower than the original idea.

Government transactions, clinics, and protected-priority queues introduce serious policy, identity, privacy, and fairness risks. The proposed first version supports only ordinary private-venue queues where handoffs are explicitly permitted.

Q-ify is not a way to bypass a line. It is a transparent handoff of an ordinary queue position where the venue allows it.

Product vision

A controlled handoff, not an unrestricted errand.

The intended product is a two-sided, location-based marketplace. Customers request permitted queue assistance, eligible runners accept tasks, and both sides coordinate queue progress and customer arrival before a verified handoff. Payments, ratings, limited evidence, and disputes may be added later.

  1. The customer books only at an approved venue and reviews its handoff rules.
  2. A verified runner checks in and posts limited status updates.
  3. The customer receives enough notice to travel before the queue reaches a nontransferable checkpoint.
  4. Both parties confirm the handoff with a rotating PIN or QR code.

What must be validated

Trust and economics come before automation.

  • Will enough customers pay a price that covers fair runner earnings, travel, support, verification, and payments?
  • Will private venues give written permission for queue handoffs?
  • Can customers arrive reliably within the handoff window?
  • Can the service operate without creating safety, labor, privacy, or anti-fixer problems?

Current implementation

Phase 1 proves one narrow backend workflow.

A caller can browse approved venues, request queue assistance for an eligible checkpoint and future time, retrieve the request, and cancel it. Venue policy rejects unsupported handoffs before a booking is stored, and repeated cancellation preserves the first result.

The implementation uses Java 21, Spring Boot, PostgreSQL, Flyway, and a modular-monolith structure. Testcontainers exercises the real database engine. A deterministic local smoke test verifies the packaged workflow, health endpoints, OpenAPI document, and cleanup; GitHub Actions repeats the verification and image build.

Current limits: there is no authentication, runner assignment, matching, payment flow, frontend, cloud deployment, or production traffic. The current lifecycle is deliberately limited to requested and cancelled bookings.

Architecture

One deployable, with boundaries that tests can enforce.

A modular monolith keeps transactions, deployment, and debugging simple while there is no evidence that independently deployed services are justified. Spring Modulith verifies the Venues and Bookings modules and their dependency direction.

Venues owns review state, checkpoints, eligibility rules, and its tables. Bookings owns the request lifecycle and calls only the exported Venues application API before persistence. It stores scalar venue identifiers rather than reading Venue repositories or tables.

Floci runs as a local AWS-compatible dependency, but the application does not use an AWS adapter yet. Its local health check is not evidence of behavior on AWS; any future adapter still requires verification against the real service.

Engineering process

Direction stays human-owned and evidence-based.

Q-ify uses an AI-assisted engineering workflow. I define and approve the product scope, architecture, constraints, acceptance criteria, and final technical direction. Coding agents support scoped implementation and review, while automated tests and CI verify accepted changes.

Next engineering milestone

Add authenticated runner assignment.

The next narrow slice is to define authenticated customer and runner actors, enforce booking ownership, and add one concurrency-safe transition from requested work to runner acceptance. It comes next because a booking cannot become marketplace work until an eligible runner can own it safely.

Duplicate-request protection, pricing, payments, notifications, live queue updates, handoff proof, a frontend, and cloud deployment remain postponed. A real marketplace launch is still separately gated by customer research, venue permission, safety, and unit economics.
